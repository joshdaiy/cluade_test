Personal Literary Essay: The Things They Carried
Ms. Airoldi

I love history and I hate fiction. That was the lie I told myself for years on end: that ever since I was a kid, all I wanted was the truth, and the whole truth. The straight line from the Battle of Varna to the rise of the Ottoman empire. The assisination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand and World War One. History, it felt like the truth. It was finished, it was done. There was nothing to change, and that's what I liked. And if the past was fixed—so was I—life was a line that was drawn from the past to the present, the present to the future. I always saw my world as a clean, unbroken line from everything that came before, a conclusion that I didn't have to defend because I couldn't change it anyways. Fiction did the opposite. It was messy, too vague. A story could end a hundred ways, but history only one: with an outcome I could predict, where I could see the inputs and outputs of every event that produced real morals, not fiction's nuanced wishes. I wanted my life to be *real*, and so I lived my life by the gospel of the past. I didn't hate fiction because it lied. I hated it because it showed me a world that I feared: a world where I wasn't in control.

So I would lie to myself, just so someone was.

It always started the same way. Something would go wrong: a bad test, a missed train, and before I could process it, I'd already written an indictment for myself. Sophomore year I bombed a chemistry quiz I'd studied a week for, and by the time I reached my locker I had the verdict: I'd rushed problem six, I'd gotten just that much less sleep, I deserved it. Never mind that I'd solved that exact reaction a hundred times. Never mind the curve, the wording, the simple fact that I'd had a bad morning. I needed a reason, so I gave myself one: my fault. I wasn't lying, I told myself, I was only a historian setting the record straight: only the small things, just to myself. I had to assign blame, because a world without blame was a world without control. Lt. Jimmy Cross does the same in Tim O'Brian's novel *The Things They Carried.* Lt. Cross is a soldier in Vietnam, but his mind is somewhere else: back home, in the past, within the confines of Martha, his girlfriend who doesn't love him back. He carries her letters, her photographs, even a pebble she sent him. He spends hours thinking about her rather than the war in front of him. So when Ted Lavender gets shot: randomly, instantly, just another casualty of war, Cross does not chalk it up to chance. Cross decides that "He felt shame. He hated himself. He had loved Martha more than his men, and as a *consequence* Lavender was now dead" (16). *Consequence,* as if it was his daydream that pulled the trigger. As a man could be loved to death. Cross searches for a cause, a line he can trace and redraw, from something he controlled to Lavander's death. Guilt was earlier than the truth, that Lavender had just died, the way men died, in a war where people died. Cross doesn't want to be innocent. Innocence is powerness. He would rather be guilty to be small, he would rather lie than tell the truth.

And so would I.

*"Lavender was dead. You couldn't burn the blame" (22)*

I don't like to admit that I am just a person. The truth I chased was one that rested on a misreading of the past: that every trouble had an explanation I could name from the facts in front of me. The real truth is more like fiction: filled with uncontrolled, hundred-ending messiness that I feared because I couldn't just flip to the present-day. I used to wish *The Things They Carried* was a history textbook. Instead it taught me the opposite of what I wanted to hear: that the human requirement for comfort and control is not fulfilled by facts but through stories built on lies, from the white lies of Tim's insistence to his daughter that he did not kill, to the lies that Jimmy Cross feeds himself in the shit field justifies the folly of my past—the past where I tried to turn history into reality through lies.

When my parents, friends, even teachers asked, about my grades, about my day, about my life, it always followed the same formula. A straight line from A to B, sanitized of disorder not because of any sort of discipline, but out of fear. "Fine," I'd say at dinner, "The quiz was easy," and the sentence would be cleaner than anything I actually felt. Slowly, the lies about my past, the "clean" version, became the only truth that I could control. To explain to them the truth would be to pretend to have something to explain, that the world bent over for a reason I could name. Yet the truth, for me, had no reason. The truth had no words. Jimmy Cross learns the same thing in the shit field, over Kiowa's death: "He would apologize point-blank. Just admit to the blunders. He would place the blame where it belonged. Tactically, he'd say, it was indefensible ground from the start… *My own fault*, he would say" (161-162). *My own fault.* Lt. Cross has no reason to blame himself; it is not just that Kiowa's father could never know the truth, but that Kiowa's death was itself, simply random. A consequence of war. His blame for himself is like mine: it's a consequence of a need to control, in an environment where there inherently is none—to explain the real truth to Kiowa's father, one that is both truthful and fulfilling to Kiowa's father, would be impossible.

*Norman Bowker looked out across the wet field. "Nobody's fault," he said. "Everybody's."*

Jimmy Cross would find that the "the exact truth" (169) for Kiowa's death in the shit field that he could tell Kiowa's father was silence. I wish that the line between my past and my present could be expressed in a letter that recounted the truth, and only the truth.

But it would have to be blank.

*Let me be blunt.* I like fiction nowadays. Creative writing. Poetry. Writing that feels like a lie. And I don't love it because it lets me reinvent my truth by rewriting my past (I have enough practice doing that\!), but because it lets me stop pretending the past was clean to begin with. I want the real truth that I hide from myself, the truth that obscures itself with my fear of the future. Function doesn't need an answer for every line. Bad quizzes are just bad quizzes. Missed trains are just missed trains. A death is just a death. O'Brian writes that: "But the thing about remembering is that you don't forget. You take your material where you find it, which is in your life, at the intersection of past and present… As a writer, all you can do is pick a street and go for the ride, putting things down as they come at you. That's the real obsession. All those stories" (33). It's the opposite of the historian I thought I could base my life around. There is a straight line from A to B, ever, even in history: we just change it to look clean.

I don't think me and Tim O'Brian have anything in common. I don't think me and Cross have anything in common, but we all want this story to be real. We want a justification—a justification for my truth and their truth that the story-truth can be more real than the truth-truth. I want the link between O'Brian's history and my truth to be as straight as the link between the Gutenberg's Printing Press and the Protestant Reformation—but *The Things They Carried* is a work of fiction, and now I enjoy it that way the best.
